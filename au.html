How the map is built

The grid is 822 by 700 cells at 0.05 degrees, about 5 km. Soil values are means of every 250 m SoilGrids pixel in the cell (or every 90 m pixel from the TERN Soil and Landscape Grid when the build has a TERN key) for 5 to 15 cm, with organic carbon at 0 to 5 cm. Climate comes from WorldClim 2.1 monthly normals, split into an April to October winter season and a November to March summer season so winter grains, summer grains and perennials are each judged on the rain they actually use.

Each crop gets a factor between 0 and 1 for seasonal rain, seasonal temperature, frost, pH, salinity and soil texture. Salinity uses the FAO yield equation, yield = 100 minus b times (ECe minus T), with ECe set to 4 dS/m where the NLWRA mapped a high salinity hazard or risk by 2050, 8 dS/m where it was mapped in 2000, and 16 dS/m where a 2000 cell is also mostly bare ground, which in the wheatbelt almost always means a salt scald or lake edge. Ultramafic belts are drawn as hatched outlines. With the serpentine option on, cells inside them cut food crops back hard and allow native nickel accumulators. The suitability score is the product of the factors. The best suggestion per cell multiplies that score by a rough value weight, so a cell that suits both wheat and saltbush shows wheat. Where satellite land cover shows a cell is under 5% cropped and mostly bush or rangeland, crops carry a further 40% discount for the clearing, approvals and infrastructure they would need, so grazing or tree crops win unless cropping is clearly better. Rain needs also rise with heat, so seasonal rain is scaled down in hot seasons and up in cool ones.

Limits worth knowing: the soil models smooth out paddock-scale variation, salinity is mapped from 2001 regional assessments, subsoil constraints such as boron and sodicity at depth are not in the grid, and seasonal normals hide drought risk. Treat the map as a first cut for where to look, then test the soil.
