    Turning marginal land productive

    Salt, sodium, serpentine, tailings, acid sulfate, sand and eroded slopes

    About 10.7% of the world's land is salt-affected and up to 40% is degraded. Some of it can be brought back into food production. Some of it is better used for crops that are not food at all: halophytes for feed and oil, and hyperaccumulator plants that mine nickel out of soils too toxic to farm.     Why spot prices and producer earnings drift apart. A supply shock raises the spot price, but a producer only keeps the part that is not eaten by its own inputs, its hedges, its contracts and its location. In 2026 the same disruption that lifts metal and fertiliser prices also raises the price of diesel, sulphuric acid and ammonia, which are the inputs that miners and fertiliser makers burn.

    How the scores work

    Supply line vulnerability = weighted sum of chokepoint risk (highest risk chokepoint on the lane), supplier concentration, 1 minus substitutability, stock thinness (1 minus cover days / 180, floored at 0) and current disruption. Hormuz dependent lanes scale their disruption and chokepoint risk down as the reopening slider rises.

    Commodity constraint score = weighted sum of seven 0 to 1 factors. The ghost tick on each bar is the score with Hormuz fully reopened. A long gap means the constraint is transient. A short gap means it is structural.

    Soil fertility and food input vulnerability are indicative teaching composites. Food input vulnerability = 0.4 x (1 minus soil index/100) + 0.3 x fertiliser import share + 0.3 x share of fertiliser imports routed through Hormuz. Replace them with ISRIC SoilGrids and FAOSTAT data for publication grade work.

    Items tagged EST are model estimates built from the cited reporting and standard reference balances. Country oil balances are approximate 2024 to 2025 pre-war values. All inputs live in data/data.js.
